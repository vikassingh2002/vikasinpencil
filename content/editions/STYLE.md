# How Agentic AI articles are written, charted and illustrated

Lessons from studying two print issues of a weekly news magazine (August 29th and July 4th 2026), recorded in our own words. Nothing here copies its text, charts or pictures; it describes the craft so our own articles can follow it.

## The kinds of article

| Kind | Length | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Leader** (opinion) | 900–1,100 words | Argues one position on the week's biggest story. Concedes a point to the other side early ("give X some credit"), then builds the case with three to five hard numbers, and ends with a prescription: what should be done, and by whom. Cross-refers to the reported pieces ("see Briefing", "see Business section"). |
| **Briefing** | 2,000–3,000 words over several pages | The week's deep dive. A big news photograph, a dateline listing the places reported from, a scene or striking fact to open, two or three numbered charts, quoted named sources, crossheads every 300–400 words. |
| **Section lead** | 1,000–1,300 words | Opens a section with a full-width picture. Starts with a vivid, specific scene (a record, an event, a person doing something) before widening to the trend. |
| **Section story** | 600–900 words | One idea, one or two charts, a pun headline and a plain standfirst. |
| **Short news analysis** | 300–500 words | One development, its numbers and its consequence. |
| **Column** | ~1,000 words, one page | Named column with its own boxed header and a conceptual illustration. Opinionated, often built around an anecdote or a historical parallel, and ending on a turn of phrase. |
| **Data story** | 800–1,000 words | Built on the magazine's own index or calculation ("we tallied", "our analysis of"). The chart is the argument: dot plots of cities, an index over time, a scatter that scores something. |
| **Essay / special package** | many pages | Long-form argument or a chronological series ("chapter 7"), each part dated. |
| **The world this week** | two pages | Politics and Business digests: one paragraph per item, the key name in bold, facts only. |
| **Obituary** | one page | One life, told through scenes, closing with a resonant detail. |
| **Economic & financial indicators** | one or two pages | Tables of figures, no prose. |

## Headlines, rubrics and openings

- **Fly title** (small, red, above the headline): a plain topic in two or three words, e.g. "Robotics", "Fiscal policy in China", "Predicting the future".
- **Headline**: short, two to five words, nearly always a pun, idiom or twist ("Shelf control", "Liquid gold", "Writing wrongs", "Falling fast fashion"). The literal meaning goes in the rubric.
- **Rubric** (bold standfirst): one plain sentence, often with a turn: "X is doing Y. But at what cost?", "If X, why Y?", "X is not the answer it seems".
- **Dateline** in small capitals above the rubric for reported pieces: the cities reported from.
- **Opening**: first two or three words in small capitals after a drop cap. Usually a concrete fact or scene with a number in the first sentence; a quotation or a provocative general statement is the alternative. The thesis arrives by the second or third paragraph.

## Inside the article

- A number in almost every paragraph, always with its unit and a comparison ("roughly seven times", "the most since 2006", "a fifth").
- Named, attributed sources: "says X, of Y", "according to Z, a consultancy". Each organisation gets a short description on first mention.
- Charts referred to in the text: "(see chart 1)", "(see chart 2, right panel)".
- Crossheads are short puns or phrases ("A big, beautiful door", "Got mulch?", "I manage, you manipulate").
- Wry asides in parentheses, understatement, no exclamation marks.
- Balance: the strongest counter-argument is given before the conclusion.
- The ending returns to the opening image or makes a sharp final judgement; the last sentence often turns a phrase. It closes with ■.
- Sections carry an "Also in this section" box listing the other stories.

## Charts

- Every chart has: a red tag at top left, a punning **title** ("Rounded up", "Training season", "Uneven bite", "Bad medicine", "Go away, young man"), a **subtitle** with geography, measure and unit ("US, monthly arrests by ICE, '000"), the y-axis scale on the right, and a **source** line at the bottom with footnote symbols (*, †) explaining estimates.
- Numbered (1, 2) in a small box when an article has more than one, and referred to in the text.
- Kinds seen, most common first:
  - **Line charts** over time, often with a shaded or annotated period (a president's term, "forecast", a policy announcement) and labels placed on the lines rather than in a legend.
  - **Column charts**: dense monthly bars for long series; a few tall bars for forecasts (estimates marked).
  - **Two panels side by side** sharing one title, each with its own subtitle.
  - **Indexed lines** (a base date = 100) to compare several things.
  - **Stacked 100% columns** comparing composition across countries.
  - **Dot plots / dumbbells** ranking many places, with a highlighted "average" row and the value in a side column.
  - **Scatter plots** with a fitted trend line, labelled outliers in boxes, and quadrant labels.
  - **Combined bars and lines** (surplus as bars, total as a line) with zero clearly marked.
- Colours: one strong red for the thing that matters, blues and greys for context; light gridlines; no 3D, no pie charts.
- Single numbers worth a sentence go in the text, not in a chart.

## Pictures

- **News photographs** for reported stories: tight, dramatic, people doing something (masked officers against a blue sky, humanoid robots seated in a row, a queue outside a bookshop). Strong colour, often shot from a low angle or with a single subject.
- **Conceptual illustrations** for leaders, columns and abstract topics: flat, bold colour, a single visual metaphor (a briefcase of oil barrels, a figure sprinting at a keyboard, a flag made of objects, scales tipping). Usually a saturated background colour.
- **Abstract scientific imagery** for science pieces (glowing physics renderings).
- Section openers use a full-width picture; inside articles, a single smaller picture or none. Captions are minimal.

## How this applies to Agentic AI

- Keep our own photographs (properly licensed) for reported pieces; prefer pictures with people or action over buildings.
- Give each article a two-to-four-word fly title, a pun headline, a plain rubric, and refer to charts by number in the text.
- Chart titles should pun; subtitles must give place, measure and unit; sources carry footnotes for estimates.
- Add new chart kinds as stories need them: two-panel charts, indexed lines, annotated time series and scatters with labelled outliers.
- Add a Leader each week, and occasionally a column or a data story built on our own calculation.
